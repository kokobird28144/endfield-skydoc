5.1 Where Do the Oscillations Come From? ​

Source page ID:0QpmucPTKcwEJ8NQjpcV4p ｜ Open in Tencent Docs

We often observe oscillations in adaptive production lines; just open the production report to see them.

(Image to be added)

The actual behavior of a production line makes this easy to understand: machines run for a while and then stop, so naturally their output rates oscillate. Yet this is still not the most fundamental explanation. Let us return to the simplest structure.

As shown above, a Depot Unloader withdraws Ferrium Ore at its full rate of 30/m. After refining it into Ferrium, half goes to a Fitting Unit to make Ferrium Parts, and half goes to a Moulding Unit to make Ferrium Bottles. Every product appears at uniform intervals: the Refining Unit outputs one Ferrium every 2 seconds; the Fitting Unit receives 1 Ferrium every 4 seconds and outputs 1 Ferrium Part every 4 seconds; the Moulding Unit receives 1 Ferrium every 4 seconds and outputs one Ferrium Bottle every 8 seconds.

Does this count as oscillation? It can. Ferrium Bottle production, for instance, alternates between running and stopping. We can understand this as two transformations of the input pattern: the two-way split at the Refining Unit output, followed by the Moulding Unit's 2:1 recipe. With an input period of 1 item every 2 seconds, 1 Ferrium Bottle emerges every 8 seconds. If the input becomes 1 item every 8 seconds, 1 Ferrium Bottle emerges every 32 seconds. These outputs clearly follow clock periods and can be classified as oscillations driven by a clock signal.

Of course, full-rate input at 1 item every 2 seconds is itself a natural clock signal, so there is nothing wrong with saying that all production lines are actually clock-driven.

For a more general explanation, introduce a production event: a machine has just completed one production process. In the example above, the Refining Unit producing 1 Ferrium or the Moulding Unit producing 1 Ferrium Bottle can each count as a production event. In production networks of this kind, with neither blocking nor closed loops, machine operation is clearly controlled directly by upstream output. When the furthest-upstream input has a constant rate (materials arrive at equal intervals), machines also start production at uniform intervals: their production events are evenly spaced in time. If the upstream input follows some changing pattern, every downstream machine develops a similar pattern. In other words, these structures do not create oscillations; they merely deliver them.

What we will discuss next is oscillation driven by blocking feedback: oscillation caused by flow changes as blocking forms and clears within a production network. A simple example is easy to construct. Add a flow limiter set to 12 per minute to the Ferrium Parts output, thereby creating blocking.

If we now observe the Ferrium Bottle output waveform, its spacing repeats as 2 slots–2 slots–3 slots: Ferrium Bottle production events repeat at intervals of 4 seconds, 4 seconds and 6 seconds, and are no longer uniform. How should we analyze this? Start at the source again: the 12/m flow limiter. We can no longer approach this from average flow rates; that tells us nothing here. What we need to know is that, with Ferrium Parts backed up behind it, the 12/m limiter actually lets 2 items through every 10 seconds. The Ferrium Parts output waveform is therefore 11000. The Fitting Unit's Ferrium input automatically becomes 11000 as well.

Next consider the Refining Unit. Its output to the Fitting Unit originally repeated as 10, but under this constraint, when Ferrium backs up in front of the Fitting Unit, the Refining Unit cannot output to it (as with a failed split). The waveform becomes 10100. Thus the Refining Unit's output toward the Moulding Unit is 01011 (the complement). Taking two periods, 0101101011, and using 2 Ferrium per 1 Ferrium Bottle, we obtain a Ferrium Bottle output waveform of 0001001001, exactly as observed.

However, analyzing and calculating digital waveforms this way is rather cumbersome and does not suit larger production networks well. We can still analyze them using the flow model introduced earlier.

Starting at the source again, interpret the 12/m limiter as a flow of 30 for 4 seconds (per-minute units are omitted below), followed by 0 for 6 seconds. During the first 4 seconds, the Fitting Unit can also be treated as consuming Ferrium at 30, while the Refining Unit can deliver Ferrium to it at no more than 15. This interval is therefore a depletion phase for the Fitting Unit's Ferrium buffer. After 4 seconds, its Ferrium consumption becomes 0, but its buffer is not yet full, so there is a filling phase for the Fitting Unit's Ferrium buffer. The Refining Unit still delivers Ferrium to it at 15. During the last part of the 10-second period, that buffer is full; all Ferrium from the Refining Unit goes to the Moulding Unit, and delivery to the Fitting Unit falls to 0.

Taking the Fitting Unit's Ferrium buffer as the variable s(t), its inflow and outflow give the following piecewise equations:

{s(t+dt)=s(t)−15/60∗dt0≤t<4s(t+dt)=s(t)+15/60∗dt4≤t<T0s(t+dt)=s(t)T0≤t<10

Let the buffer capacity be C. Since the buffer is at capacity at both the beginning and the end of each period, the solution is straightforward:

{s(t)=C−0.25t0≤t<4s(t)=C−1+0.25(t−4)4≤t<T0s(t)=CT0≤t<10

Matching the pieces also gives T0=8: depletion lasts 4 seconds, filling lasts 4 seconds, and blocking persists for the final 2 seconds. We can therefore write the Ferrium flow v1 toward Ferrium Parts production and directly calculate the Ferrium flow v2 toward Ferrium Bottle production:

v1(t)={0.250≤t<808≤t<10v2(t)={0.250≤t<80.58≤t<10

The expression for v2 immediately tells us how Ferrium Bottles are produced.

Although this method has various issues arising from converting a discrete process into a continuous one, it is sufficient for analysis at the event scale of interest here. We can immediately see that t=4 in s(t) corresponds to the minimum of the Ferrium-buffer oscillation, C-1, and that for 2 seconds out of every 10 seconds, the Refining Unit sends Ferrium to the Moulding Unit at full capacity. This agrees with the waveform analysis. We can of course also average v1 and v2 over time, obtaining 12 and 18, recovering the ordinary flow-balance solution that ignores the fine details.
