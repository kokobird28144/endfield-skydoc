3.1 The Beginnings of Automatic Balancing: Xiranite and Originium Powder’s Love–Hate Relationship ​

Source page ID: KHooQBQESX74amkILMa8UI | Open in Tencent Docs

Xiranite and Originium Powder will find their way out.

—— Perlica, inspecting the adaptive designs in Wuling, July 2026

Also known as “A simple blocking-based balancing design with two inputs and two products.”

Origins ​

Everything began with a simple observation: Xiranite and Dense Originium Powder can be made into different products at different ratios:

10 Xiranite+10 Dense Originium Powder (Packed Origocrust)=1 Xiranite Component5 Xiranite+15 Dense Originium Powder=1 LC Wuling Battery

Since Packed Origocrust is obtained from Originium Powder through just two direct refining steps, we can essentially treat them as the same thing.
An almost reflexive conclusion follows: provided the incoming Xiranite-to-Originium-Powder ratio lies between the ratios of these two recipes, a linear combination must exist that consumes all the inputs. In other words, once the input quantities are given, solving a system of equations tells us that making X Xiranite Components and Y LC Wuling Batteries consumes all the raw materials and balances production.

But Endfield machines cannot have their production rates controlled directly. We can only control their rates indirectly by controlling the quantities of inputs supplied. This led to the earliest, simplest exact-ratio splitting scheme: solve the equations for the required ratios of the two inputs, then construct splitter networks to feed the specified flow ratios into the machines. This stage produced substantial theory about constructing splitters—which of course is still developing today—see Compiling General-Purpose Splitter Theory.

Version 1.2 brought Purification Units and the Purification Node, making recipe calculations more complicated. Exact solutions of the linear equations were often complex fractions, particularly difficult to construct and install. Was there a better approach?

Consider another angle. In recipe balancing, what we want is to consume all incoming materials into products. If we can achieve that by another means, we need not calculate exact flows; we can start directly from conservation, bypassing recipe-ratio calculations.

If you have tinkered with enough production lines, you have probably noticed that a Packaging Unit that is not producing often has material A waiting for material B. Its buffer is full of A; B is consumed as soon as it enters, and A production continues filling the machine. Mirror this arrangement, and a surprising result appears: if one machine is full of A and the other full of B, when each material goes to the other machine, it can immediately be consumed because the other material is already full. With this design, the input branches for A and B seem never to block, since there is always an open route. If they do not block, all incoming materials must be consumed. The output ratio must therefore be the one corresponding to full consumption, achieving adaptive balancing. Priorities are what initially fill the two machines with A and B.

Derivation with Priorities ​

In the terminology of production controlled by blocking:

  1. For material A, machine M is the controlled branch and machine N the free branch.

  2. For material B, machine N is the controlled branch and machine M the free branch.

Every material thus has a free branch, so it cannot block during normal operation. Every material also has a controlled branch, whose consumption depends on the supply of the other material through its free branch.

Does this really converge? Here is the answer first, with the reason afterward: supply a material first to the recipe consuming relatively less of it, and second to the recipe consuming relatively more of it.

Again take 12 Xiranite + 30 Originium Powder making small batteries and Xiranite Components. For simplicity, call the Packed Origocrust needed for components “powder” as well. Note that powder supply over any given time is always 2.5 times Xiranite supply.

  1. Initially, starting empty, small batteries consume relatively more powder, so powder first goes to equipment components and Xiranite first to batteries. For convenience, assume the relevant buffers in both machines become full simultaneously. (They need not; it barely affects the reasoning.)

  2. Because full buffers block, Xiranite starts entering the Gearing Unit and powder the Packaging Unit. At the supply rates, once 15 powder / 6 Xiranite have entered, production of the first LC Wuling Battery begins, consuming 5 Xiranite / 15 powder.

  3. Next, 5 Xiranite continues to refill the Packaging Unit first. During this, 12.5 Originium Powder enters the same unit; the Gearing Unit still waits.

  4. When the Packaging Unit reaches 15 Originium Powder, it makes a battery as in step 2. During this process, 1 Xiranite overflows to the Gearing Unit, which now holds 7 Xiranite.

  5. In this small cycle, each small battery causes 1 Xiranite to overflow to the Gearing Unit. Once overflow reaches 10, the Gearing Unit starts and makes 1 component.

  6. Refilling then follows priorities again. By the time 5 Xiranite enters the Packaging Unit, 10 powder has entered the Gearing Unit and 2.5 Xiranite has entered the Packaging Unit.

  7. By the time 12.5 powder enters the Packaging Unit to make a battery, 5 Xiranite has entered the Gearing Unit.

From here, you should be able to see a stable cycle forming.

  1. Again assume the Packaging Unit's powder and Gearing Unit's Xiranite become full simultaneously.

  2. Powder overflows to the Gearing Unit. Once 10 enters, it begins making 1 equipment component, consuming 10 Xiranite and 10 powder. Meanwhile, 4 Xiranite overflows to the Packaging Unit.

  3. Xiranite continues refilling the Gearing Unit first, but after just 4 Xiranite, 10 powder has entered and starts the next component. The Gearing Unit loses a net 6 Xiranite; nothing happens in the Packaging Unit.

  4. Repeating this cycle—receive 10 powder and 4 Xiranite, make a component—eventually leaves only 4 Xiranite in the Gearing Unit.

  5. When another 10 powder enters, Xiranite reaches only 8. Its supply in the Gearing Unit is now completely controlled by powder: as soon as Xiranite reaches 10, it is consumed into a component. Meanwhile, powder keeps accumulating inside the unit.

  6. Eventually powder fills the Gearing Unit and blocks. Since the Packaging Unit is also full of powder and blocked, the immediate powder source blocks too.

Input is now blocked and the production objective is not met. Actual production becomes 12 Xiranite + 12 powder, all into equipment components, while the surplus 18 powder cannot enter production.

If powder originally entered the Packaging Unit more slowly, so Xiranite entered the Packaging Unit before being consumed, the outcome differs. Once Xiranite enters it, every 5 Xiranite consumes 15 powder; replenishing 15 powder admits 6 Xiranite, so Xiranite accumulates until blocked. Actual production becomes 10 Xiranite + 30 powder, all into batteries, while the surplus 2 Xiranite cannot enter production.

The importance of priority should now be apparent. For the more general recipes

K11 material A+K12 material B=product 1K21 material A+K22 material B=product 2

we state that supplying A first to product 1 and B first to product 2 yields stable balancing if and only if the input supply ratio lies between the recipe ratios (the linear system has a positive solution), and

K11K22<K12K21

If the inequality does not hold, supply A first to product 2, effectively interchanging A and B.

This can be derived by modeling changes in flow (see the paper PDF). You can also reason through these K-coefficient recipes; the proof is left to the reader—I expect that should work too.

Using Splitting Ratios ​

Instead of priorities, consider the splitting-ratio concept in 2.5 Is a Stash Not Just a Special Splitter? (Core Content ⭐). Assume the stability conditions above hold. Let RA be the fraction of A sent to product 1 and RB the fraction of B sent to product 2. RA=RB=1 corresponds to the priority case above. What ranges of RA and RB achieve automatic balancing?

Here is the conclusion directly. From the actual input quantities I1,I2, calculate the exact splitting ratios RA*,RB*. As long as RA and RB lie in the rectangle formed by that exact-splitting point and the priority point, automatic matching of production is achieved.

RA>RA∗RB>RB∗

There are also two degenerate cases: RA=RA* or RB=RB*. In the former, both machines are already at their maximum capacities consistent with exact splitting for material 2; in a sense, this degenerates into single-input blocking-based balancing for material 2.

From section 2.5, these two oversupply conditions establish the correct blocking states in both machines and thus the production objective. This is indeed another way to derive the conditions, but a rigorous mathematical derivation is fairly cumbersome; see the paper PDF.
