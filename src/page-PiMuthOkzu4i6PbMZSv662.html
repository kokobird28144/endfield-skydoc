3.3 You... You Xiranite-Freeloading Pig! ​

Source page ID: PiMuthOkzu4i6PbMZSv662 ｜ Open in Tencent Docs

Version 1.4 introduced Solid-Gas and Fluid-Gas Transmuting Units. Both require at least 6/min Xiragen or Liquid Xiranite to remain operating (activated). They consume it even when starved of inputs or blocked by full products. More supplied Xiragen/Liquid Xiranite means faster consumption, up to 30/min, without any performance improvement over 6/min: pure Xiranite theft.

It can actually be regarded as a special form of power supply.

Endfield/AIC: 1.4 Miser's Tip 2—Limit Liquid Xiranite to 6 for an Acridgen Environment? No, Only 1.2

Let me see how many people's first thought was limiting Liquid Xiranite to 6... (Mine too.)
This pig steals so much food [doge].

Initial investigation found the actual mechanism: each Xiragen or Liquid Xiranite entering lets the machine work for 10 seconds—5 runs of a 2-second recipe or 1 run of a 10-second recipe. We can therefore expand the original recipes, for example:

5 Xiranite+1 Xiragen (activation)→5 Xiragen10 Hetonite Gas+5 Xiragen+1 Acridgen (activation)→5 Pyrrolite Gas (single-core Pyrrolite)

These expanded recipes let us calculate balanced production and plan lines.

But! Writing recipes like this implicitly assumes:

If any input is missing, no reaction occurs and no other input is consumed.

This is false for activation gas/liquid. The machine consumes activator whether it works or not. Without controlling this consumption, much activator inevitably fails to produce according to the recipe, making the result incorrect.

First consider the simple solution: use limiters for fixed consumption (obviously 6 is best; more gives no benefit). Activator consumption is then fixed-rate, rather than proportional to the recipe. We can still calculate the final production plan. Accounting for this fixed consumption reduces the machines to ordinary machines, letting us use the preceding two sections' automatic-balancing methods.

If we additionally require no activator waste, as maximum output demands, activator must be supplied according to input or product quantity. The preceding sections' balancing can achieve this, but here the major problem is:

Xiragen/Liquid Xiranite activator cannot directly be a controlled branch.

Why? A controlled branch must block, causing waste. Could it be a free branch? If there is only one, yes: other materials fill up, activator is limited to 6, and each arrival makes the corresponding product quantity. But the whole process has several Xiragen/Liquid-Xiranite-activated transmuters. They cannot all be free branches: Xiranite allocation among them would be indeterminate—all to one, halved, etc.—and would not match production states.

Clearly, using the preceding sections' balancing in these complex lines requires activation branches to be controlled branches. Yet they must not block. How can we reconcile this? Based on input/product quantities, create additional control artificially, forming blocking midway along the path from Liquid Xiranite/Xiragen to activation, then using input/product quantities to govern subsequent activator release. From the Liquid Xiranite/Xiragen perspective, these activation branches still act as blocked controlled branches, allowing blocking-based production principles to apply.

Control can take feedforward and feedback forms. For Fluid-Gas transmutation of Cuprium Solution into Cuprium Gas, feedforward controls Liquid Xiranite by incoming Cuprium Solution quantity; feedback controls it by departing Cuprium Gas quantity. Figuratively:

Feedforward: whenever five Cuprium Solution passengers board at the starting station, dispatch one Liquid Xiranite driver; at the terminus, unload five Cuprium Gas passengers.

Feedback: after five Cuprium Gas passengers leave at the terminus, the empty vehicle teleports to the starting station and boards five Cuprium Solution passengers and one Liquid Xiranite driver.

The idea initially used direct ratio control rather than the current fluid-gas feedback scheme: allocate activator proportionally to materials. Two direct implementations (by me, 君, 智乃, etc.) are:

  1. Where Liquid Xiranite activates its own conversion into Xiragen, send 1/6 of the input directly. To avoid phase or splitting-failure effects, sometimes reduce this to 1/8 or similar, filling buffers first and limiting the terminal to 6 for reliability.

  2. Use Separating/Filling Units to detect passing input quantity, take the corresponding proportion of bottles, fill them with Liquid Xiranite, then separate them, introducing 1 Liquid Xiranite.

These proportional methods barely use blocking or local adaptation; in a sense they are closer to exact-ratio splitting. At least they work locally. Applying them to a whole line revealed new problems:

Under feedforward control (inputs control activator), inputs cannot be a blocked controlled branch.

Similarly, under feedback control (products control activator), inputs cannot be a flowing free branch.

Why? First, if inputs are controlled, their initial flow exceeds downstream acceptance and eventually blocks. Downstream blocking propagates upstream, blocking products and then inputs. Even after downstream blocking clears, the transmuter cannot recover automatically: our rule admits Liquid Xiranite only after five inputs board, but the input buffer is full. Emptying it requires Liquid Xiranite to activate consumption; yet inputs must first be consumed before five more can enter to admit Liquid Xiranite. The line locks in a full-storage state.

Second, if inputs are free, their initial flow is low, at least below full rate. Five departing products trigger feedback and 1 more drop of Liquid Xiranite, but sufficient buffered inputs are not guaranteed. Feedback admits one activator whenever five products leave, regardless of buffer contents. Eventually inputs are insufficient and Liquid Xiranite fails to produce five products. Thereafter Liquid Xiranite requires Cuprium Gas and Cuprium Gas requires Liquid Xiranite: the line locks in an empty-storage state.

Combined feedforward/feedback schemes now exist, but we leave them aside here.

My version 1.4 maximum-gross-output design essentially combines the preceding two sections' balancing principles with these two rules.

Pyrrolite is Cuprium's low-priority/free branch and Heavy Xiranite its high-priority/controlled branch. Along the Cuprium → Pyrrolite Gas/Pyrrolite route, Cuprium must therefore use feedforward control. Likewise, Cuprium → Heavy Xiranite and Xiranite → Pyrrolite need no special control, because these routes basically have no fluid-gas transmutation issue. (The gasification on the Xiranite → Pyrrolite route is directly included in the later Xiranite → Heavy Xiranite route.) Theoretically Xiranite → Heavy Xiranite also needs feedforward control, but in practice a small proportional split suffices.

In the following video I also use Separating/Filling-Unit-based feedback for Acridgen. Feedforward is avoided mainly because this design combines a fixed 3 Acridgen portion and a variable automatically balanced portion. Feedforward would also need to add the fixed acid-liquid supply; feedback simply detects it directly.

Later, 言 launched the “Wild—Shake All of Asia!” project, developing fluid-gas feedback adaptive devices with 君bear and others. Traditional proportional schemes allocate strictly by ratio. Fluid-gas feedback adaptation instead creates two ratios (e.g. 5 inputs to 0.5 activator, 1:0.1; 5 inputs to 1 activator, 1:0.2), switches between them through blocking signals, and controls those signals through remaining inventory. This feedback yields stable oscillation: the proportion of operating/stopped time adaptively produces the required ratio.

These methods better demonstrate automatic convergence in blocking-based production: create two easy endpoints enclosing the target, then control switching conditions between their production states so that the long-term average automatically converges to the target. This idea extends further: e.g. Solid-Item Feedback / Flow Regularization, automatically switching low/high settings to create large full-to-empty oscillations, or even control energy levels.

For engineering implementations of fluid-gas feedback devices, see Some Simpler Adaptive Setups.
