2 Basic Concepts and Simple Examples of Blocking-Based Production ​

This section introduces basic concepts and simple examples. Understanding it lets you design blocking-based production for multiple finished products from a single input material, meeting most simple automatic-production needs. If you believe you already know this material, read Section 2.5 directly; if you understand it, proceed straight to Section 3.

One point worth making at the outset: discussing blocking-based production usually involves two stages, theoretical planning and engineering implementation. In theoretical planning, we discuss which finished products to make, how much input is available, which processing steps are required, material routing and allocation, and priority design. For example, demand analysis may tell us to assign X of 120 Ferrium to powder production and Y to Yazhen Syringe [C], but we are not yet especially concerned with how to build a line grinding X Ferrium.

In engineering implementation, we discuss facility layout, the particular structures implementing priority, and structural optimization. With sufficient theoretical planning, implementation usually amounts to wiring the planned connections. Optimizing those connections is outside this article's focus, though it still takes considerable skill. Most subsequent analysis therefore emphasizes theoretical planning; commonly used implementation structures appear in the appendix.

Long-Period Reaction Oscillation ​

This topic requires either of two premises (feasibility: 1>2):

1. The production side's range of adaptation to inputs covers the oscillation between both states. 2. Additional buffering lets machines consume stored inputs during accumulation, and excessive machine input does not waste material. (There is a risk of the control being overwhelmed.)

We have three signal-detection methods: the full-storage signal and empty-storage signal, which detect depot inventory, and a manually controlled clock signal. Pairing them produces long-period oscillations that keep inputs in storage. This reduces the additional unloaders needed for balancing with empty storage, and also reduces losses where direct full-storage overflow, affected by the withdrawal waveform, prevents material being deposited at its origin.

Full-Storage–Clock Control ​

Design sequence: detect failure to deposit into storage (full) → admit reaction inputs and increase consumption → store the signal in a clock and delay it → stop the reaction and begin accumulation. This is one operating cycle.

Following the preceding discussion, add clocks both before and after blocking. This effectively controls accumulation duration T1 s and overconsumption duration T2 s. The controlled factory consumes at v unit/min. T1 and T2 can be changed through the clock structures and contents. For identical structures, assuming a constant signal-propagation delay t s, consumption is vT2/(T1+T2+t) unit/min, allowing some degree of p/q consumption limiting.

Generating Periodic Point Signals ​

As shown, Basic Factory Principles allows one Ferrium Ore output every 100 seconds.

Define a downstream-flowing logistics stream called a point-signal stream. Once stable, every downstream output interval is a multiple of the largest possible fixed interval T, where T is quite large. The Ferrium Ore line above is plainly such a stream, with T = 100s. Call T its clock period and 1/T its clock frequency f, here 10mHz.

Clock Sampling ​

Feedback requires converting the source's flow state into digital signals 0 and 1, with 0 meaning inactive and 1 active. One idea is to regularize the source output, concentrating its output within each clock period into the same part of that period, for example:

The result in Logistics Principles and Frontier Research lets us construct the illustrated device to meet this requirement.

Sampling one point in a clock period then compares the source's total output over an interval with a set value: greater output gives sample 1; otherwise 0. The sample point's position within the clock period determines this threshold.

Signal Operations ​

Protocol Stash priorities permit logical operations on point signals, following exactly the simple-logic rules in Basic Factory Principles. Here 1 means the point-signal stream outputs one item in the clock period, and 0 means no item.

Take particular care: if no AND follows the final NOT, the resulting signal is an almost-full complementary belt and fails the point-signal requirement. Add an AND with a high-level signal.

Signal Reconstruction ​

After logical operations, convert the result into output. Here an output signal means downstream will not continuously receive reaction inputs in the current period. The illustrated device performs this function:

When Ferrium Ore representing the computed output enters the Protocol Stash, the stash blocks and batteries cannot pass through. Its complementary belt reaches the stash output after a one-period delay. If the computed signal has stopped, Ferrium Ore exits, unblocking the stash, and batteries output continuously during the next period. Otherwise Ferrium Ore enters and exits simultaneously, leaving one Ferrium Ore in the stash; blocking persists and batteries cannot output.
