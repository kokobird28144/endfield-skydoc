2.5 Is a Stash Not Just a Special Splitter? (Core Content ⭐) ​

Source page ID: o3yxqfGNALKamNYPO1eu1t ｜ Open in Tencent Docs

Original section title: Replacing Output Priority with Splitting Structures; Splitting Ratios and Calculating Adaptive Ranges

Switching-type blocking uses priority to achieve switching and generally does not involve this section's allocation of numerical flow rates. This section mainly concerns balancing-type blocking.

Output-priority schemes are reliable, but their structural design is cumbersome. For solids in particular, one belt carries only 30 items/min, making priority handling at high flow rates troublesome.

Example ​

Below, we want two Cuprium streams to prioritize Cuprium Canisters, then make Cuprium Bottles. Calculations give downstream demand of 22/m Separator Cores (an example, represented by a limiter; actually constrained by the downstream line), requiring 44/m Cuprium. A priority design therefore needs two stashes separately controlling the streams' priorities, followed by merging. Besides complexity, crossings can change priority and must be handled.

Is there a simpler way? Yes, brother, yes.

Recall fixed-ratio splitting: split each stream at 11/30. But constructing that fraction is too troublesome. Try ordinary halving Splitters.

Why? Like a stash, a Splitter creates paths from one source to two downstream machines. The Moulding Unit consumes at most 44 Cuprium/min. Once full and blocked, will the remainder not automatically divert to Cuprium Bottles? The structure is much simpler too!

Reality is harsher. This cannot achieve our target. Obviously the two Moulding Units each consume 30 Cuprium, producing 15 canisters and 15 bottles. What went wrong?

Clearly, the Moulding Unit did not block properly. In the priority example it initially receives 60 Cuprium but can consume only 44 Cuprium, so its rate falls from 60 to 44 and stays at 44. Only then does the remaining 16 flow divert to Hetonite Bottle production.

How do we fix it? A Splitter cannot initially give all flow to Cuprium Canisters, but supplying more than 44 Cuprium will also make it block and stay at 44, will it not? If halving fails, use 2/33/4. Consider:

Each Cuprium branch is halved twice: 1/4 enters the Cuprium Bottle Moulding Unit and 3/4 the Cuprium Canister unit. Initially, the Cuprium entering canister production runs at 45, exceeding the canister consumption rate 44 and causing blocking. The Cuprium stream to bottles, initially 15, naturally becomes 16 after the canister unit blocks, achieving our target. Splitters and Convergers also allow more flexible placement than stashes. Often one halving split solves it.

Clearly Cuprium Canisters are the controlled branch, and bottles the free branch. Thus, to replace stash priority with a splitting structure, design the split so that the controlled branch's allocated flow exceeds its calculated required production-input rate, making it block. The remaining flow then diverts to free-branch production.

This already demonstrates local oversupply.

Splitting Ratios ​

We can now discuss the splitting ratio. In the middle example, the halving Splitter gives the branches 0.5 and 0.5 of the original flow (here full rate 30) before blocking forms. In the final example these fractions before blocking forms are 0.25 and 0.75. In the first, priority-based example, they are actually 1 and 0 before blocking forms. A unified model therefore describes how upstream flow divides between two downstream branches: their fractions before blocking are R and 1-R. R=0 or 1 represents output priority; a fractional R can be constructed with suitable splitting structures Compiling General-Purpose Splitter Theory. For theoretical discussion, use R alone, without distinguishing a priority-output device from a Splitter/Converger network.

I think R should by default mean the fraction sent to the controlled branch, since that is our first product of interest. This convention applies below unless otherwise stated.

Given R (e.g. 0.25/0.75) and one belt's Cuprium input (30), the two flows before blocking are 7.5 and 22.5. Call the controlled-branch flow 22.5- and the free-branch flow 7.5+, distinguishing their roles and indicating that rates during blocking-based (adaptive) production are not fixed and may change. As noted initially, blocking causes redistribution. Once the controlled branch is constrained and blocking forms, its flow must be below 22.5 and the free branch's above 7.5. Exact values depend on how the controlled branch is controlled. This notation can simplify some production-line calculations.

For example, Xiranite at 15- and Origocrust at 10+ can balance in Xiranite Component production at a rate between 10 and 15: Xiranite blocks and fills the Gearing Unit, so its actual consumption depends on Origocrust supply. Xiranite at 15+ and Origocrust at 10-, however, cannot give the desired stable flows. Origocrust supplies at most 10; Xiranite blocks at only 10 and fails to act as a free branch. The free branch now also blocks, and Xiranite accumulates in the depot.

Finally, does the line still achieve its intended requirements when input supply or production demand changes? This determines its adaptive range.

Theoretically, the only thing we designed here is R. Let R be the input fraction sent to Cuprium Canisters, and replace the Cuprium supply rate 60 with I. Calculated canister consumption may also change with other material supplies; denote it P*. The blocking condition becomes:

IR>2P∗(2.5.1)

In the example, 2P*=44: 601=60>44, 600.5=30<44, 60*0.75=45>44. Designs 1 and 3 therefore achieve the blocking-production target.

Furthermore, define:

R∗=2P∗/I(2.5.2)

The condition simplifies to:

R>R∗(2.5.3)

What is R*? Consider the symbols: it is exactly the ratio required for exact-ratio (fixed-ratio) splitting! This reveals the connection between exact splitting and blocking-based (adaptive/overflow) production. If designed flow to the controlled branch exceeds the calculated exact-split flow, blocking forms correctly. Equation 2.5.3 is the most important equation in all of Section 2 on single-input blocking-based production.

The adaptive range is the range of input supplies and production demands over which a fixed design (R) works. For fixed P*, all supply rates above 2P*/R work; for fixed I, all production demands below IR/2 work. This determines the adaptive range. Actual machine production limits must also be considered, of course. Occasionally I and P* vary together; then examine the range of R*=2P*/I and judge them jointly.

R therefore affects adaptive range. R=1, strict priority, minimizes the required lower bound on supply and increases the upper bound on production demand. Replacing the R value with Splitters, to some extent

Some may find the constant 2 puzzling. It comes from the recipe: 2 Cuprium + 1 Inergen → 1 Cuprium Canister , the input quantity per product. Different recipes naturally change it. This value matters in Section 3.

Dynamic analysis can prove that R>R* is the condition for local dynamic stability of the target production flow under blocking.
