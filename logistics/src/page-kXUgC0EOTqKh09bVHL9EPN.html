Xiragen: Fluid-Gas Transmuting Unit — Feedforward with Feedback Control ​

Production recipe: 1 Liquid Xiranite (activation) + 5 Liquid Xiranite = 5 Xiragen

P.S. Although the machine UI lists a “minimum rate of 6 units/min” for the activation material, each unit actually keeps the machine running for 10 s. There are exactly three situations in which activation material is wasted: ① the machine is active but lacks inputs; ② the machine is active but its output is blocked; ③ two units of activation material arrive less than 10 s apart.

Feedforward: the input supply rate determines the machine's operating rate.

Feedback control: the machine runs at a high rate when the input material overflows, and at a low rate when it does not.

P.S. Don't worry if feedforward, output-driven control, ratio control, and feedback control don't make sense yet. Keep reading and use the actual machines to build an understanding of these categories.

The priority created by the “Conduit Outlet Manifold–Pipe Converger” arrangement shown above (Input, Output, and Merge Priority Theory) sends Liquid Xiranite into the machine's material input first. Only after the machine fills up does Liquid Xiranite start entering the activation port. In other words, whenever activation material enters, sufficient input material is already available. Provided that the output remains unblocked—all the Xiragen produced is consumed downstream—and the activation supply does not exceed 6/min, no activation material will be wasted. A Pipe Control Port set to 6/min provides the required limit. [TN1]

When the input material—Liquid Xiranite—overflows, some of it reaches the activation port and the machine runs at its high rate of 30/min. If the incoming Liquid Xiranite supply is below 36/min, the machine consumes the accumulated material until it no longer overflows into the Conduit Outlet Manifold. Once that overflow stops, Liquid Xiranite no longer reaches the activation port, so the machine runs at its low rate of 0/min. If the incoming supply is above 0/min, material then accumulates until it overflows into the manifold again. This cycle repeats until, over a sufficiently long period, the amount of Liquid Xiranite buffered in the setup neither increases nor decreases. Here, “buffer” simply means the Liquid Xiranite retained within the setup. This is what we mean by “feedback control.” Or, for an input supply of 0/min–36/min: input overflows → the machine speeds up → input stops overflowing → the machine slows down → input overflows again, and so on. [TN2]

Because the buffered Liquid Xiranite neither increases nor decreases over a sufficiently long period, the rate entering the setup equals the rate consumed by the machine. Viewed from the input side, the supply rate therefore controls the machine's operating rate. Looking at the actual sequence, every 1 Liquid Xiranite entering the activation port allows the machine to consume 5 Liquid Xiranite, since sufficient material is always available. Another 5 Liquid Xiranite must then enter the material input before another unit can reach the activation port. If the incoming supply rate is v1, the machine's actual reaction rate—its Xiragen output rate—is:

v1/(5+1)*5

Reading the numbers from left to right: 5 Liquid Xiranite enter the material input, 1 enters the activation port, and 5 Xiragen are produced. Once again, the input supply rate controls the machine's operating rate. This is what we mean by “feedforward.”

At this point, the last two paragraphs may feel like a long way of stating the obvious. That's because this setup is simple enough to make the explanation seem redundant. If feedforward, feedback control, or even output-driven control becomes confusing later, come back to this simple case and use it to work through the more complicated ones.

The Conduit Outlet Manifold in the image is also labelled “Avoid connecting to a Conduit Inlet Manifold.” Connecting a manifold inlet to a manifold outlet allows liquid or gas transfer at 2–4/s. However, the “Conduit Outlet Manifold–Pipe Converger” priority arrangement sends any flow above 2/s into the lower-priority pipe, so its priority is not absolute. For example, suppose the instantaneous flow is “1/s for 1 s, 2/s for 1 s, then 3/s for 1 s.” Its average is 2/s, so you would expect nothing to enter the lower-priority pipe. In fact, during the second at 3/s, the priority pipe takes 2/s and the lower-priority pipe takes 1/s. The lower-priority pipe unexpectedly receives an average of 1/3/s! Connecting a single-port conduit endpoint to a manifold instead caps the flow at 2/s, giving the arrangement strict priority.

For now, call this the input-phase problem of manifold–Converger priority. Later sections call it the phase problem.

Xiragen: Fluid-Gas Transmuting Unit — Feedforward with Ratio Control ​

Production recipe: 1 Liquid Xiranite (activation) + 5 Liquid Xiranite = 5 Xiragen

Feedforward: the input supply rate determines the machine's operating rate.

Ratio control: directly maintain a 5:1 ratio between input material and activation material.

When using this setup, cap the incoming supply at 36/min—the actual flow may be below 36/min—or cap the Fluid-Gas Transmuting Unit's activation input at 6/min.

The splitting arrangement shown above sends five times as much Liquid Xiranite into the material input as into the activation port. Because the recipe uses the same material for both purposes, this is the simplest case of ratio control. The activation flow is held at a fixed ratio to the material input flow, so the latter controls the former. Naturally, the input supply rate then controls the product output rate, or the machine's operating rate. That is “feedforward.”

Cuprium Gas: Fluid-Gas Transmuting Unit — Feedforward with Feedback Control ​

Production recipe: 1 Liquid Xiranite (activation) + 10 Cuprium Solution = 5 Cuprium Gas

Feedforward: the input supply rate determines the machine's operating rate.

Because of the priority created by the “Pipe Converger–Pipe Splitter” arrangement shown above (Input, Output, and Merge Priority Theory), when the incoming Cuprium Solution supply is between 30/min and 60/min, Cuprium Solution fills the machine before Liquid Xiranite increases its flow into the activation port from 3/min to 6/min. This means sufficient material is available whenever activation material enters. Provided that the output remains unblocked—all the Cuprium Gas produced is consumed downstream—and the activation supply remains below 6/min, no activation material will be wasted. A Pipe Control Port set to 6/min provides the limit. [TN1]

Why does an incoming Cuprium Solution supply of 30/min–60/min ensure sufficient material whenever activation material arrives? When Cuprium Solution fills the machine and backs up into the conduit buffer, maintaining a blocked state, the converger accepts only the higher-priority Cuprium Solution. Liquid Xiranite cannot enter, so its recirculation is blocked. With that return path blocked, all 6/min of Liquid Xiranite enter the activation port, and the machine consumes Cuprium Solution at 60/min. If the incoming supply is below 60/min, the amount of Cuprium Solution in the setup gradually falls until it no longer occupies all the converger's input opportunities. Liquid Xiranite can then recirculate normally, reducing its activation flow to 3/min. The machine now consumes Cuprium Solution at 30/min. If the incoming supply is above 30/min, Cuprium Solution accumulates again until it occupies the converger and blocks Liquid Xiranite recirculation once more. This repeats until, over a sufficiently long period, the amount of Cuprium Solution buffered in the setup neither increases nor decreases. The same approach can of course be used to solidify Heavy Xiranite, Hetonite, or Pyrrolite: only the input and activation materials change. This is “feedback control” for a setup whose input material and activation material are different. Or, when the incoming material supply is below 30/min: input overflows → the machine speeds up → input stops overflowing → the machine slows down → input overflows again, and so on. [TN2]

Since the buffered Cuprium Solution neither increases nor decreases over a sufficiently long period, its incoming supply rate equals its consumption rate. The input supply rate therefore controls the machine's operating rate: “feedforward.”

For a single fluid, you can use a splitter–converger arrangement directly: for example, diverting 1/5 of the Liquid Xiranite to activate a Fluid-Gas Transmuting Unit, or diverting 1/11 of the Xiragen to solidify purified Heavy Xiranite. In general, the flow being measured must be returned to its original production line after measurement, so most of these approaches copy the signal. Think of it as making an impression: start with a template, let X items of A leave X empty spaces in it, then use those spaces to receive X items of B. Our main task is to build that template using the game's mechanics.

Feedforward Using Filling and Separating Units — Both Fluids Must Be in the Same Phase ​

Both must be liquids, or both must be gases.

The following setup controls the ratio of Liquid Xiranite to Cuprium Gas at 1:10. [TN4] Cuprium Solution exits the conduit outlet at the upper right. Half goes directly into the transmuter; the other half enters a Filling Unit to be measured. The Filling Unit has plenty of empty bottles, so every unit of Cuprium Solution entering it produces one bottle of Cuprium Solution. That bottled solution immediately enters the next Separating Unit, which extracts the liquid and returns it to the Fluid-Gas Transmuting Unit.

From that unit of Cuprium Solution's perspective, all it has done is take a detour. So what has changed? The Separating Unit has released an empty bottle! It has five connected outputs: four return bottles directly to the stash, while the fifth sends bottles into a Filling Unit full of LIQUID! XIRANITE! A bottle takes one unit of Liquid Xiranite and goes into another Separating Unit. Think back through the chain: each bottle taking this route means five bottles have left the first Separating Unit, which means five units of Cuprium Solution entered the first Filling Unit, which means ten units of Cuprium Solution left the conduit outlet in total. Thus, for every 10 units of Cuprium Solution, exactly 1 unit of Liquid Xiranite enters the Fluid-Gas Transmuting Unit, supplying precisely what is needed.

For more examples, see my version 1.4 automatic-balancing plan video. These mainly include Cuprium Solution feedforward control, which measures Cuprium Solution; Pyrrolite solidification feedforward control, which measures Hetonite Gas; and an Acridgen feedback-control setup, which also measures Hetonite Gas.

Because Filling and Separating Units consume quite a lot of power and take up considerable space, most later designs used the other mechanisms on this page. Still, I think filling-and-separating ratio control may be the easiest approach for beginners to understand. It also uses relatively few pipe components, comes with a built-in five-way splitter, does not depend on priority, runs as precisely as meshing gears, responds quickly, and has small oscillations.

Pyrrolite: Multiple Solid-Gas Transmuting Units — Feedforward with Feedback Control ​

Treat several machines as a single system. For example, regard three Solid-Gas Transmuting Units as one machine consuming up to 90/min of Pyrrolite Gas as input and 18/min of Xiragen for activation, then connect a feedback-control module. [TN6]

Make sure input material accumulates during low-rate operation. Consumption by units not directly connected to the control module must be less than or equal to consumption by those directly connected. When Xiragen is split equally among the three activation ports, this means the input supplied to the units not directly connected must be greater than or equal to the input supplied to the directly connected units. You do not have to split activation material equally, provided the consumption condition above is met; in this three-unit case, however, an unequal split would not save any more pipe components. Isolated Control? Centralized Control! Equal-Split Control?! ​

With the following arrangement, the feedback cycle moves from the machine alone—“input overflows → speed up → input stops overflowing → slow down → input overflows again”—to the Conduit Outlet Manifold and the machine together. The supported flow can clearly reach 240/min, allowing more units to be connected. Isolating the control circuit therefore lends itself naturally to centralized control of multiple units.

But is 240 the upper limit? Of course not! Consider the feedback arrangements for multiple Solid-Gas and Fluid-Gas Transmuting Units. To keep flow low enough to reuse a splitter, only the supply to some of the machines passes through the control module. Likewise, we can draw, say, 30 from each of several 240 streams, merge those portions, and pass them through an isolated control module. This lets us control all the modules through equal splitting. In practice, one control module can serve enough units to consume up to 120/min of activation material. More than 120/min is possible too, but that level of demand seems unlikely in the foreseeable future: 1200 Cuprium Solution input, plus the obvious demand for solid Cuprium, would far exceed even the Ferrium Ore supply in the valley.

Xiragen: Solid-Gas Transmuting Unit — Output-Driven Control Using Overflow ​

kokobird: Boss, here's a contribution.

未来科技's requirement: the upstream line supplies 18 Xiragen, while downstream demand varies between 18 and 48. The Solid-Gas Transmuting Unit needs to start and stop on demand. [TN5]

How it works: the external gas supply first enters a recirculation loop. One branch shares a pipe with any other fluid; the mixed-fluid section must be more than 1 grid cell long. When the other fluid is flowing, it blocks the shared pipe. Xiragen first accumulates in the Gas Tank, then overflows into the Solid-Gas Transmuting Unit, which starts operating normally. Total output now reaches 48. Because downstream demand is below 48, material gradually backs up until it blocks the other fluid. The Xiragen on the left can then take both routes, so the overflow stops. Eventually, the Solid-Gas Transmuting Unit stops as well, returning output to 18. Output is now insufficient for downstream demand, so the other fluid eventually starts flowing again. This creates an adaptive oscillation cycle.

The drawbacks are the usual ones for overflow designs: slow startup, slow response, and long cycles. Let the upstream flow be I and downstream demand be O’. The actual total demand is O, given by O'+(O'-I)*1.25. Let downstream storage capacity be C. Startup takes approximately 500/(I/2), after which Xiranite production begins. A further C/(30+I-O) elapses before the other fluid is blocked. If this takes too long, the Conduit Outlet Manifold fills with C0 Xiragen. Recovery after blocking then takes C0*10(~5000s), and the extra Xiranite production driven by this Xiragen reaches C0*10(~5000s)*(30+I-O'). A fairly large buffer is needed. [TN8]

Translator's notes

These notes identify ambiguities in the source. They are not corrections to the design or independent verification of its mechanics.

TN1 — Activation timing and the 6/min limit. The opening distinguishes average supply from the interval between activation events, but later states that a Control Port set to 6/min is sufficient. That assertion is retained as the author's claim; a mean rate alone does not establish event spacing. In the Cuprium Gas section, the source also says “below 6/min” while recommending a limit of exactly 6/min. The strict inequality is retained. TN2 — Input ranges. The current first Xiragen section uses 0/min–36/min. The Cuprium Gas section develops a 30/min–60/min safe range, but its grey summary says “below 30/min.” Both ranges are translated as written rather than silently reconciled. TN3 — The single-fluid split. The overview says to divert 1/5 of Liquid Xiranite for activation, whereas the earlier recipe and ratio-control example specify 5 units for production plus 1 for activation. The intended denominator is not explained there; 1/5 is retained. TN4 — Cuprium Gas versus Cuprium Solution. The filling-and-separating section initially states a 1:10 ratio between Liquid Xiranite and Cuprium Gas.
